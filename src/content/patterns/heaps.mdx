## The idea

A heap gives you the extreme element in O(1) and maintains that property in
O(log n) per change. It does **not** give you sorted order, arbitrary lookup,
or deletion of a specific element — and knowing what it cannot do is what
tells you when to reach for something else.

Three uses cover nearly every question.

## Top k — and the inversion that catches people

<Snippet file="code/heaps.py" symbol="k_largest" title="Keep k, evict the weakest" />

To keep the **largest** k you use a **min**-heap. That feels backwards for
about ten seconds, and then it is obvious: the root of a min-heap is the
weakest survivor, so it is exactly the thing you want to evict when a better
candidate arrives.

O(n log k) against O(n log n) for a full sort. When k is small — and it
usually is — that is a real win, and it is the reason the interviewer asked
for *k* rather than "sorted".

The alternative is [quickselect](../divide-and-conquer/) at O(n) expected.
Offer it: "if I only need the k largest as a set and not in order,
quickselect is O(n) average, though O(n²) worst case." That comparison is the
answer to "can you do better than n log k".

<Snippet file="code/heaps.py" symbol="k_closest_to_origin" title="k closest — two details" />

Two things worth saying while writing it: compare **squared** distances, since
`sqrt` is monotone and costs precision for nothing; and to keep the k
*smallest* you now need a **max**-heap, which in Python means negating.

## Merging k streams

<Snippet file="code/heaps.py" symbol="merge_k_sorted" title="One candidate per list" />

The heap holds only the current head of each list, so it never exceeds k
entries — that is what makes it O(N log k) rather than O(N log N). Pushing
everything and sorting is simpler and usually acceptable; the heap is the
answer when the lists are streams you cannot materialise.

The tuple needs the list index as a tie-break, or Python will try to compare
the payloads when two values are equal and raise a `TypeError`. That crash is
the most common bug in this problem.

## Two heaps facing each other

<Snippet file="code/heaps.py" symbol="MedianFinder" title="Running median" />

The idiom worth memorising. A max-heap holds the smaller half, a min-heap the
larger half, and the median is at their boundary. Keep the sizes within one of
each other.

**The one-line insert** is the part that looks like magic:
`heappush(low, -heappushpop(high, value))`. Pushing through the *other* heap
first is what guarantees correctness — a value must be compared against the
opposite side before it settles, or you can end up with a large value stuck in
the small half.

## What a heap cannot do

This is the most useful thing on the page, because it tells you when to stop.

- **No arbitrary deletion.** You cannot remove a specific non-root element in
  O(log n) with `heapq`. The standard workaround is **lazy deletion**: push
  `(value, expiry)` and discard stale roots on pop. That is how the
  [skyline problem](../intervals/) and sliding-window-median get solved.
- **No neighbour queries.** "The largest key at most X" needs an
  [ordered set](../ordered-set/), not a heap.
- **No sorted iteration.** `heap[1:]` is not sorted; only `heap[0]` is
  meaningful.

## Pitfalls

- **`heapq` is min-only.** Negate for a max-heap, and negate again on the way
  out. Say you are doing it, because silently negated values are hard to read.
- **Tuple comparison crashes** on unorderable payloads. Add an index
  tie-break.
- **`heapify` is O(n)**, not O(n log n). Building from a list is cheaper than
  n pushes, and knowing that is a nice detail.
- **`heappushpop` versus `heapreplace`.** The first pushes then pops (so the
  new item can be returned immediately); the second pops then pushes. They
  differ when the new item is smaller than the root.
- **Reaching for a heap when a sort would do.** If the whole collection is
  available up front and you need everything in order, sort it.

## Anchor walkthroughs

### K Closest Points to Origin

**Clarify.** Is the answer order significant? (Usually not.) Are duplicates
possible? Is k guaranteed at most n?

**Three solutions, in increasing sophistication** — give the ladder, it reads
much better than jumping to one:

1. Sort by distance, take k. O(n log n).
2. Max-heap of size k. O(n log k).
3. Quickselect. O(n) expected, O(n²) worst.

**The detail.** Squared distance, no `sqrt`.

**When to prefer which:** heap when k ≪ n or the input is a stream;
quickselect when everything is in memory and you want the best average; sort
when n is small and clarity wins.

### Find Median from Data Stream

**Clarify.** Are values integers or floats? Bounded range? Is `findMedian`
called after every insert or occasionally? (If values are bounded small
integers, a counting array beats both heaps — worth asking.)

**The naive answer.** Keep a sorted list and insert with `bisect`: O(log n) to
find the position but O(n) to shift. Say this and why it fails.

**The insight.** You do not need the whole thing sorted — only the boundary.
Two heaps hold the two halves and the median sits at their meeting point.

**Dry-run** 1, 2, 3, 0. After 1: low = [1], median 1. After 2: low = [1],
high = [2], median 1.5. After 3: low = [1,2], high = [3], median 2. After 0:
low = [0,1], high = [2,3], median 1.5. Check the rebalance fires on the third
insert.

**Follow-ups they actually ask:** "all values are in [0, 100]" → counting
array, O(1) insert, O(100) median. "99% of values are in [0, 100]" → counting
array plus two overflow lists. Both reward asking about the range up front.

### Task Scheduler

**Clarify.** Must the output be an actual schedule, or only its length? (Only
the length, which is what makes the formula possible.) Can tasks be reordered
freely? Is the cooldown between *identical* tasks only?

**The trap.** It looks like a simulation with a heap and a cooldown queue —
and that works, and is a fine answer. But there is a closed form, and finding
it is the point.

**The insight.** The most frequent task defines the skeleton: `maxCount - 1`
gaps, each of length `cooldown + 1`, plus the tasks tied for most frequent at
the end. If there are enough other distinct tasks to fill every idle slot,
there is no idling at all and the answer is just `len(tasks)`.

**That `max(len(tasks), skeleton)`** is the whole insight compressed into one
line. Explain both branches: the skeleton dominates when one task is very
frequent, `len(tasks)` dominates when the work is spread out.

**Dry-run** `["A","A","A","B","B","B"], n = 2` → skeleton = 2·3 + 2 = 8.
Then `["A","A","A","B","C","D","E","F","G"], n = 2` → skeleton = 2·3 + 1 = 7,
but there are 9 tasks, so the answer is 9. The second case is the one that
catches a formula written without the `max`.
