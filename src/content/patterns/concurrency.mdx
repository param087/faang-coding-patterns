## The idea

Asked far more often than candidates prepare for — and the bar is low, which
makes it excellent value. LeetCode has only nine concurrency problems; doing
all nine takes an afternoon and covers the category.

One rule answers most of them:

> **A lock protects state. A semaphore counts permits. A condition lets a
> thread sleep until a predicate holds. A barrier makes threads wait for each
> other.**

And one thing is never the answer: **busy-waiting.** Spinning on a boolean
burns a core, gives no ordering guarantee, and tells the interviewer you have
not used threads in anger.

## Ordering with semaphores

<Snippet file="code/concurrency.py" symbol="Foo" title="Print in Order" />

Two semaphores initialised to **zero** act as one-shot gates: `second` blocks
until `first` releases it.

Why semaphores rather than locks: a `Lock` is conceptually owned by whoever
acquired it, and releasing another thread's lock is at best confusing. A
semaphore is explicitly a *signal between threads*, which is what this is.

<Snippet file="code/concurrency.py" symbol="FooBar" title="Strict alternation" />

Strict alternation needs **two gates facing each other**, each thread
releasing the other and then waiting to be released. One shared lock is not
enough — nothing stops a thread from reacquiring it immediately and printing
twice in a row. That is the failure mode to be able to name.

## The classic: a bounded blocking queue

<Snippet file="code/concurrency.py" symbol="BoundedBlockingQueue" title="One lock, two conditions" />

One lock guards the deque; two conditions let threads sleep until the queue is
not full or not empty.

**`wait_for` re-checks the predicate on wake, and that is the point.** Writing
`if not full: wait()` instead of a loop is *the* bug this question exists to
find. Two reasons it breaks:

- **Spurious wakeups** are permitted by the underlying primitives.
- **Another thread may win the race** after you are notified but before you
  reacquire the lock, so the condition you were waiting for is false again.

`wait_for(predicate)` is the loop, written correctly. If you write it by hand,
write `while not predicate: cond.wait()`.

## Grouping

<Snippet file="code/concurrency.py" symbol="H2O" title="Building H2O" />

Threads must leave in groups of exactly two hydrogen and one oxygen. Semaphores
alone let a hydrogen thread rush ahead and form HHH; a `Barrier(3)` encodes
"nobody proceeds until the group is complete", which is precisely the
requirement.

The semaphores still matter — they cap how many of each type may be *waiting*
at the barrier at once, so the groups form correctly rather than arbitrarily.

## Python specifics worth knowing

- **The GIL** means threads do not give you CPU parallelism for pure Python.
  They do give concurrency for I/O, and they are still the right tool for
  these problems, which are about *coordination*. If an interviewer asks about
  CPU-bound parallelism, the answer is `multiprocessing`.
- `threading.Lock` is not reentrant; `RLock` is. Acquiring a `Lock` twice in
  the same thread deadlocks instantly.
- `with lock:` is always better than manual acquire/release — it releases on
  exception.
- `Condition` needs a lock; passing the same lock to two conditions (as above)
  is what lets them coordinate.

## Pitfalls

- **`if` instead of `while`** around a wait.
- **Busy-waiting.**
- **Deadlock from lock ordering.** If two threads take locks A then B and B
  then A, they will eventually deadlock. Fix by imposing a global order on
  lock acquisition — which is exactly the standard Dining Philosophers
  solution.
- **Forgetting to release on the exception path.** Use `with`.
- **Assuming notify wakes everyone.** `notify()` wakes one; `notify_all()`
  wakes all. Using `notify` when several threads wait on different predicates
  can wake the wrong one and stall.
- **Testing once and declaring it correct.** Race conditions are
  probabilistic. Run it many times, and say that you would.

## Anchor walkthroughs

### Print in Order

**Clarify.** Are the three methods called from three separate threads? In an
arbitrary order? Is each called exactly once?

**The naive attempt, and why it fails.** A shared boolean plus a spin loop
"works" and is wrong: it burns CPU and, on a weak memory model, offers no
guarantee the write is visible. Naming that is worth more than the solution.

**The insight.** Two gates, each initialised to zero, released in sequence.

**Test it by starting the threads in the wrong order** — third, second, first.
If the output is still correct, the synchronisation is real rather than
accidental. The test in the module does exactly this.

**Follow-up:** generalise to n methods in order — an array of n−1 semaphores.

### Design Bounded Blocking Queue

**Clarify.** Should `enqueue` block when full, or throw? (Block.) Is
`dequeue` blocking? Multiple producers and consumers, or one each? (Multiple —
which is why `wait_for` and not `if`.)

**The structure.** A deque, one lock, two conditions.

**The `while` versus `if` argument**, stated explicitly. This is the single
thing the question is testing.

**Which condition to notify.** `enqueue` notifies `not_empty`; `dequeue`
notifies `not_full`. Notifying the wrong one is a stall that only appears
under load.

**Follow-up:** what if you need a timeout? `wait_for(predicate, timeout=...)`
returns a boolean rather than blocking forever — a good answer, and a real
production concern.

### The Dining Philosophers

**Clarify.** Five philosophers, five forks, each needs both neighbours' forks.
Must the solution be starvation-free, or merely deadlock-free? (Ask — they are
different guarantees and the second is easier.)

**The deadlock.** If every philosopher picks up their left fork simultaneously,
all five hold one fork and wait forever for the other. Describe this before
proposing a fix; it is the reason the problem exists.

**Three standard fixes**, and being able to list them is the answer:

1. **Impose a global order** — every philosopher picks up the lower-numbered
   fork first. This breaks the circular wait, which is one of the four
   necessary conditions for deadlock.
2. **Limit concurrency** — a semaphore permitting at most four philosophers to
   try at once, so at least one can always eat.
3. **Asymmetry** — odd-numbered philosophers pick up left first, even ones
   right first.

**Name the four Coffman conditions** if you can — mutual exclusion, hold and
wait, no preemption, circular wait — and note that every fix above removes one
of them. That framing is what turns a puzzle answer into a systems answer.
