## The idea

A greedy solution is **worthless without a reason it is optimal**. Interviewers
know that greedy is where candidates guess, so the question after your code is
always some form of "how do you know that works?"

The reason is almost always an **exchange argument**:

> Take any optimal solution. Show that swapping its first choice for the
> greedy choice leaves it no worse. Repeat. Therefore the all-greedy solution
> is optimal.

If you cannot construct that argument, the problem is probably
[dynamic programming](../dp-1d/) and greedy will fail on some case you have
not thought of.

## One variable instead of a table

<Snippet file="code/greedy.py" symbol="can_jump" title="Jump Game" />

The DP answer is `reachable[i]` for every index — O(n) space, O(n²) time in
the naive form. The greedy is one variable: track the furthest index reachable
so far, and if the loop ever stands beyond it, there is a gap nothing crosses.

**Why it is safe:** reachability is monotone. If you can reach index i, you
can reach everything before it, so a single frontier captures all the
information a table would.

<Snippet file="code/greedy.py" symbol="min_jumps" title="Jump Game II — BFS without a queue" />

This is a **breadth-first search written without a queue**. `current_end` is
the boundary of the current jump — the end of the current BFS level — and
reaching it means one more jump has been committed. `farthest` is the boundary
of the next level.

Seeing it as BFS is what makes it obviously correct, and it is a better
explanation than "we jump when we have to".

## The two-pass rule

<Snippet file="code/greedy.py" symbol="candy" title="Candy — constraints in both directions" />

Worth studying because it is where single-pass greedy **fails**.

The constraint points both ways: a child must have more than their left
neighbour *and* more than their right one. No single sweep can satisfy both,
because when you are going left to right you cannot yet see the right
neighbour's final value.

Two passes fix it. Left to right handles the left constraint; right to left
handles the right one, taking a `max` so the first pass is not undone. That
`max` is the entire subtlety — plain assignment breaks `[1,3,2,2,1]`.

**The transferable lesson:** when constraints point in two directions, do two
passes and combine.

## When the greedy is a formula

<Snippet file="code/greedy.py" symbol="task_scheduler" title="Task Scheduler" />

Sometimes the greedy collapses into arithmetic. The most frequent task defines
a skeleton of `(maxCount − 1)` gaps of length `cooldown + 1`, plus the tasks
tied for most frequent. If there are enough other tasks to fill every idle
slot, there is no idling and the answer is simply `len(tasks)`.

`max(len(tasks), skeleton)` handles both regimes in one line, and being able
to explain *both branches* is what makes it a real answer rather than a
remembered formula.

## Pitfalls

- **Greedy that is not optimal.** Coin Change with arbitrary denominations is
  the standard counterexample: greedy fails on coins `[1, 3, 4]` and amount 6
  (greedy gives 4+1+1, optimal is 3+3). Have it ready — it is the answer to
  "why not greedy here?"
- **No exchange argument.** If you cannot produce one, say so and reach for DP
  rather than hoping.
- **Sorting by the wrong key.** Interval scheduling sorts by *end*, not start.
  See [Intervals](../intervals/).
- **Missing the second pass** when constraints are bidirectional.
- **Local optimum ≠ global.** The whole risk of the category, in four words.

## Anchor walkthroughs

### Jump Game II

**Clarify.** Is reaching the end guaranteed? (In Jump Game II, yes — otherwise
it is Jump Game I.) Can jumps be zero-length? Is the answer the count of
jumps, not of indices?

**The DP solution first.** `dp[i]` = fewest jumps to reach i, filled by
relaxing from every earlier index. O(n²), correct, and it establishes the
baseline.

**The insight.** It is BFS by levels: from everything reachable in k jumps,
compute everything reachable in k + 1. Track the two boundaries and you never
need a queue.

**Dry-run** `[2,3,1,1,4]`. Level 0 is index 0. From it you reach up to index 2
— that is level 1. From indices 1 and 2 you reach up to index 4 — level 2.
Answer 2. Point at where `i == current_end` fires, because that is the line
doing all the work.

**Why the loop stops at `len(nums) - 1`:** arriving at the last index does not
require another jump, and including it over-counts by one.

### Gas Station

**Clarify.** Is the answer guaranteed unique? (Yes.) Is the circuit
clockwise? Can values be negative individually? (The differences can.)

**Two independent facts**, and stating them separately is what makes the
one-pass solution obvious:

1. If total gas < total cost, **no** start works. Conservation of fuel.
2. If you run dry partway from `start`, **no station between `start` and the
   failure point works either** — each would begin with even less fuel in the
   tank. So the next candidate is the station *after* the failure.

Fact 2 is the one that turns O(n²) into O(n), and it is the part interviewers
want articulated.

**Dry-run** `gas = [1,2,3,4,5]`, `cost = [3,4,5,1,2]`. Totals are equal, so an
answer exists. Starting at 0: −2, then −4 — fail at index 1, so restart at 2.
From 2: −2, fail at 2... trace it properly on the board; the point is watching
`start` jump forward rather than incrementing.

### Candy

**Clarify.** Must every child get at least one? (Yes.) Are equal ratings
required to get equal candy? (No — equal neighbours have *no* constraint
between them, which surprises people and is worth confirming.)

**The failed single pass.** Try left-to-right only on `[1,3,2,2,1]` and watch
it produce too few candies for the descending tail. Showing the failure is
worth more than asserting that two passes are needed.

**The insight.** Two passes, one per direction, combined with `max`.

**Complexity.** O(n) time, O(n) space.

**Follow-up:** O(1) space. It exists — count the lengths of ascending and
descending runs and use the triangular-number formula — and it is
significantly fiddlier. Mention it exists; attempt it only with time to spare.
