## The idea

A small category, disproportionately common at Google and Meta, and unusual in
one respect: **the interviewer usually wants a proof of uniformity**, not just
working code. "It looks random" is not an answer. Have the induction ready.

## Weighted selection

<Snippet file="code/randomized.py" symbol="WeightedPicker" title="Prefix sums plus binary search" />

Prefix sums turn weights into contiguous segments on a number line: weight 1
owns `[0, 1)`, weight 3 owns `[1, 4)`. A uniform draw into that line lands in
a segment with probability exactly proportional to its length, and
[binary search](../binary-search/) finds which one.

O(n) to build, O(log n) per pick.

**`bisect_left` against the inclusive prefix totals is the correct bound.**
Using `bisect_right` shifts every pick by one and quietly gives the weight of
index i to index i+1 — a bug that produces plausible-looking output and fails
a distribution test.

## Reservoir sampling

<Snippet file="code/randomized.py" symbol="reservoir_sample" title="Sampling a stream of unknown length" />

The one to actually know. You must sample k items uniformly from a stream
whose length you do not know and cannot store.

Keep the first k. For each later item i (0-based), keep it with probability
`k/(i+1)`, replacing a uniformly chosen survivor.

**The proof, which is what they are asking for.** Assume inductively that
after processing i items, every one of them is in the reservoir with
probability `k/i`. Now item i arrives:

- It enters with probability `k/(i+1)` — correct by construction.
- An incumbent survives if either the new item is rejected, or it is accepted
  but evicts one of the *other* k−1 slots. That is
  `1 - (k/(i+1)) · (1/k)` = `i/(i+1)`.
- So an incumbent's overall probability is `(k/i) · (i/(i+1))` = `k/(i+1)`.

Every item has the same probability, and the induction closes. Being able to
walk through those three lines is the whole question.

<Snippet file="code/randomized.py" symbol="LinkedListSampler" title="k = 1, the case that hides" />

Linked List Random Node is reservoir sampling with k = 1, and the phrasing
hides it. "You don't know the length and can't store the list" **is** the
reservoir condition — recognising that is most of the solve.

## Shuffling

<Snippet file="code/randomized.py" symbol="shuffle" title="Fisher-Yates" />

Swap position i with a uniformly random position in **`[i, n)`**.

The common wrong version draws from `[0, n)`. It looks fine and it is
provably biased: it generates `n^n` equally likely swap sequences over `n!`
permutations, and since `n!` does not divide `n^n`, some orderings are
strictly more likely than others.

That counting argument is exactly the answer to "how do you know your shuffle
is uniform", and it is why the question is asked.

## O(1) insert, delete and random

The RandomizedSet problem lives on the [Design](../design/) page because the
interesting part is the structure pairing rather than the randomness. The
trick is worth repeating here: **to remove an element in O(1), swap it with
the last one and pop**, keeping the array dense so uniform indexing stays
valid.

## Pitfalls

- **`random.randint(a, b)` is inclusive at both ends**; `randrange(a, b)`
  excludes `b`. Mixing them is an off-by-one in a distribution, which no test
  case will make obvious.
- **Drawing from `[0, n)` in Fisher-Yates.**
- **`bisect_right` in weighted picking.**
- **Reseeding inside the loop**, which destroys independence.
- **Claiming uniformity without an argument.** If you cannot prove it, say
  that you would verify empirically — running a million draws and checking the
  histogram is a legitimate answer and better than a hand-wave.
- **Rejection sampling that may not terminate.** `rand10` from `rand7` is
  expected O(1) but unbounded in the worst case; say so.

## Anchor walkthroughs

### Random Pick with Weight

**Clarify.** Are weights positive integers? Can they be huge? How many picks
relative to the number of weights? (Many picks means the O(n) build amortises
away, which justifies the preprocessing.)

**The insight.** Prefix sums map weights onto a line; a uniform draw plus
binary search selects proportionally.

**Dry-run** `w = [1, 3]`. Prefix `[1, 4]`. A draw in `[0, 1)` picks index 0
(probability 1/4); a draw in `[1, 4)` picks index 1 (probability 3/4).
Checking the boundary at exactly 1 is what confirms the bisect variant.

**Complexity.** O(n) preprocessing, O(log n) per pick.

**Follow-up:** what if the weights change? Then the prefix array is O(n) to
rebuild, and you want a [Fenwick tree](../segment-tree/) over the weights,
with the pick becoming a descent through it in O(log n).

### Linked List Random Node

**Clarify.** Do I know the length? (No — that is the constraint.) Can I
traverse twice? (If yes, count then index, and the problem is trivial. The
interesting version says no.) Is the list mutable between calls?

**The easy answer first.** Two passes: count the nodes, pick an index, walk to
it. O(n) time, O(1) space. Correct — and it is worth giving, because it makes
the constraint that rules it out explicit.

**The reservoir answer.** One pass, replacing the current choice at node i
with probability `1/(i+1)`.

**The proof, out loud.** Node i is chosen at step i with probability `1/(i+1)`
and then survives every later step j with probability `j/(j+1)`. The product
telescopes: `1/(i+1) · (i+1)/(i+2) · … · (n-1)/n` = `1/n`. That telescoping is
elegant and short enough to say — it is the best possible answer to "why is
this uniform".

**Complexity.** O(n) per call, O(1) space.

### Insert Delete GetRandom O(1)

**Clarify.** Are duplicates allowed? (The follow-up variant allows them and
changes the structure substantially.) Must `getRandom` be uniform over
*values* or over *occurrences*? Does `insert` return whether it was new?

**The tension**, stated first: a hash set gives O(1) membership but no uniform
pick; a list gives O(1) pick but O(n) removal. Naming why neither suffices is
what motivates the answer.

**The insight.** Keep both — a list of values and a map from value to its
index — and remove by **swapping with the last element and popping**. No
shifting, so O(1), and the array stays dense so indexing stays uniform.

**The detail that breaks it:** after the swap you must update the map entry
for the element that *moved*, not just delete the one that left. Forgetting
that leaves a stale index and the next removal corrupts the array.

**Dry-run** insert 1, insert 2, remove 1. The 2 moves into slot 0 and its map
entry must follow it. Walk that explicitly — it is the whole bug surface.

**Follow-up:** duplicates allowed. The map becomes value → *set* of indices,
and removal picks an arbitrary one. Still O(1), and noticeably fiddlier.
