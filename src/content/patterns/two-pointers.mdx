## The idea

Three distinct techniques share this name, and choosing between them is most
of the work.

| Shape | Pointers | Needs | Typical problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Converging | both ends, moving inwards | sorted input | 3Sum, container with most water |
| Same direction | read and write, both forward | a keep/drop rule | remove duplicates, move zeroes |
| Fast & slow | one moves twice as fast | a sequence that may loop | cycle detection, middle of list |

The unifying idea is an **invariant that lets you discard a candidate
permanently**. Every one of these is O(n) because each step eliminates
possibilities you never have to revisit — and being able to say what is being
eliminated is what the interviewer is listening for.

## Converging

<Snippet file="code/two_pointers.py" symbol="three_sum" title="Sort, fix, converge" />

The algorithm is easy; the **deduplication is the whole difficulty**, and it
is where most attempts fail. Two separate skips are needed:

- Skip a repeated *fixed* element (`nums[i] == nums[i-1]`), or you emit the
  same triple family twice.
- After recording a hit, skip repeats of the left pointer, or `[0,0,0,0]`
  yields `[0,0,0]` several times.

The early `break` when `nums[i] > 0` is not just an optimisation — on a
sorted array nothing further can sum to zero, and saying so shows you are
reasoning about the sorted structure rather than just using it.

Sorting costs O(n log n) and the scan is O(n²), so the sort is free. That is
worth stating: it explains why you can afford to sort at all.

## The exchange argument

<Snippet file="code/two_pointers.py" symbol="max_area" title="Always move the shorter wall" />

This one is short but is really a proof question. Why is it safe to discard
the shorter wall?

Area is `width × min(left, right)`. Moving either pointer shrinks the width.
If you move the *taller* wall, the height is still capped by the shorter one,
so the area cannot increase — that option is strictly dominated. Only moving
the shorter wall gives any chance of a taller minimum. So the discarded
pointer can never be part of a better answer.

Interviewers ask "how do you know you didn't skip the optimum?" almost every
time. Having that paragraph ready is the difference between a pass and a
hesitant maybe.

Three regions, one pass, O(1) space. The subtle line is the `2` branch: after
swapping with `high`, **`i` does not advance**, because the value pulled in
from the right has not been examined. Advancing it there is the classic bug,
and it fails on `[2, 0, 2, 1, 1, 0]` — which is exactly why that is the
sample input.

The follow-up is always "now find where the cycle *starts*". After the

they meet at the entrance. Know the result even if you cannot re-derive the
algebra on the spot.

## Pitfalls

- **Forgetting to sort** when the technique needs sorted input. Converging
  pointers are meaningless on unsorted data.
- **`while left < right` versus `<=`.** For pair-finding you want `<`, so an
  element cannot pair with itself. For binary-search-style scans you often
  want `<=`. Decide deliberately.
- **Deduplicating in the wrong place.** Skip *after* recording, not before,
  or you drop legitimate answers.
- **Mutating while scanning.** In the write-pointer shape, be sure you have
  read a value before you overwrite its slot.
- **Sorting destroys original indices.** If the answer needs indices, sort
  `(value, index)` pairs or use a hash map instead.

## Anchor walkthroughs

### 3Sum — the dedup problem in disguise

**Clarify.** Are duplicate *triples* allowed in the output? (No — that is the
entire difficulty.) Can I sort, or does the answer need original indices?
May the same element be reused?

**Brute force.** Three nested loops, O(n³), plus a set to dedupe. At n = 3000
that is 2.7·10¹⁰. Dead on arrival, and the constraint says so.

**The insight.** Fix one element and the problem becomes 2Sum on a sorted
array, which converging pointers solve in O(n). Total O(n²), which at n =
3000 is 9·10⁶ — comfortable.

**Write the dedup deliberately.** Talk through both skips as you write them
rather than adding them after a failing test; that reads as understanding
rather than debugging.

**Dry-run** `[-1, 0, 1, 2, -1, -4]` → sorted `[-4, -1, -1, 0, 1, 2]`. i = 0
finds nothing. i = 1 (-1) finds `[-1, -1, 2]` and `[-1, 0, 1]`. i = 2 is
skipped as a duplicate fixed value — point at it and say so, because that is
the line under test.

**Follow-up:** 3Sum Closest (track the best difference instead of exact
zero), or 4Sum (one more loop, same dedup discipline).

### Container With Most Water — proving the discard

Covered above. The interview shape: give the O(n²) brute force, state the
exchange argument *before* writing the loop, then write six lines. If you
write the code first, the interviewer will ask for the proof anyway, and
producing it under pressure is harder than leading with it.

### Sort Colors — the one-pass constraint

**Clarify.** "Can I do two passes?" — counting sort is trivial: tally 0s, 1s,
2s, then overwrite. The interviewer will say one pass, which is the point.

**The insight.** Maintain three regions with two boundaries and a scanner.
Everything left of `low` is 0, everything right of `high` is 2, and the
middle is being resolved.

**The detail that decides it** is the non-advancing `i` on the 2-swap. Dry-run
`[2, 0, 1]` explicitly: if you advance `i` you get `[1, 0, 2]`, which is
wrong, and the bug survives most other inputs.

**Follow-up:** k colours instead of 3. The two-boundary trick does not
generalise, so it becomes counting sort — knowing where a technique stops is
worth as much as knowing where it works.
