## The idea

Choose, explore, un-choose. Every problem in this category is those three
lines wrapped in a loop.

<Snippet file="code/backtracking.py" symbol="subsets" title="The template" />

What varies is only:

- **Where you record an answer.** Subsets record at *every* node; permutations
  record only at leaves.
- **Where the loop starts.** `start` (combinations, order irrelevant) or 0
  with a `used` array (permutations, order matters).
- **What you prune.** This is the part that decides whether it passes.

`path[:]` matters. Appending `path` itself stores a reference that the next
`pop()` mutates, so every entry in the result ends up empty. It is a one-
character bug with a spectacular failure mode.

## The two dedup rules, which are not interchangeable

This trips people up more than the recursion does.

**Duplicate values in the input** — sort, then skip repeats at the same depth:

<Snippet file="code/backtracking.py" symbol="subsets_with_duplicates" title="Skip repeated values at the same level" />

The condition is `i > start`, not `i > 0`. The first occurrence at each level
must be allowed through; only the second and later are duplicates *of a choice
already made at this depth*. Writing `i > 0` silently drops valid answers.

**Each index used once** — that is the `used` array in `permutations`, and it
is a different mechanism for a different problem. If the input has duplicates
*and* order matters, you need both.

## Pruning is the whole game

<Snippet file="code/backtracking.py" symbol="combination_sum" title="Sort to enable an early break" />

Two prunings here, and the sort is what enables the second:

- `if candidates[i] > remaining: break` — on sorted input, everything after is
  also too big, so `break` rather than `continue`.
- Passing `i` instead of `i + 1` permits reuse. That single character is the
  entire difference from the subsets template.

<Snippet file="code/backtracking.py" symbol="solve_n_queens" title="O(1) conflict checks" />

N-Queens is where pruning becomes structural. Scanning the board to test a
placement is O(n) per attempt; three sets make it O(1).

The observation that does it: on a `\` diagonal, `row - col` is constant. On a
`/` diagonal, `row + col` is constant. So a diagonal is just an integer key.
Deriving that on the whiteboard — rather than recalling it — is what the
question is really testing.

## Pitfalls

- **`path` instead of `path[:]`.**
- **Forgetting to un-choose.** Every `append` needs its `pop`, every `add`
  its `discard`. Write them as a pair, immediately.
- **`i > 0` instead of `i > start`** in the dedup.
- **Not sorting** before a dedup-by-adjacency, which then does nothing.
- **Claiming a complexity that ignores the copy.** Subsets is O(n · 2ⁿ), not
  O(2ⁿ), because each of the 2ⁿ answers costs O(n) to copy out.
- **Backtracking when DP applies.** If the question asks *how many* or *what
  is the best*, and subproblems repeat, it is [DP](../dp-1d/). Backtracking
  is for enumerating the solutions themselves.

## Anchor walkthroughs

### Subsets

**Clarify.** Are values distinct? (If not, it is Subsets II and needs the
dedup.) Does the output order matter? Must the empty subset be included?
(Yes.)

**Why it is asked.** It is the template in its purest form, and the
interviewer wants to see the choose/explore/un-choose rhythm written without
hesitation.

**Two solutions worth having.** The recursive one above, and the
[bitmask](../bit-manipulation/) one: count from 0 to 2ⁿ − 1 and read each
integer as a membership set. Offering the second shows you see the
correspondence between subsets and binary numbers.

**Complexity.** 2ⁿ subsets, each costing O(n) to copy: O(n · 2ⁿ).

**Follow-up:** Subsets II, which is exactly the dedup rule above.

### Combination Sum

**Clarify.** May a number be reused without limit? (Yes — Combination Sum II
says no, and that is the `i` versus `i + 1` difference.) Are candidates
distinct? Positive? (Positivity is what makes the `break` sound — with
negatives there is no monotone cutoff and the search may not terminate.)

**The insight.** A search tree where each node either takes the current
candidate again or moves on. Sorting enables the early break.

**Dry-run** `[2,3,6,7]`, target 7. Take 2 → remaining 5 → take 2 → remaining 3
→ take 2 fails (2 > ... no, 2 ≤ 3) → take 2 → remaining 1, then 2 > 1 so
break, and 3 > 1 so this branch dies. Back up: 2,2,3 sums to 7 ✓. Eventually
7 alone ✓. Result `[[2,2,3],[7]]`.

**Follow-up:** Combination Sum II — each candidate used once, and duplicates
in the input. Now you need *both* `i + 1` and the sorted-skip dedup, which is
a good demonstration that the two rules are independent.

### N-Queens

**Clarify.** Return the boards or just the count? (N-Queens II wants the
count, which lets you skip building strings.) Is n small? (n ≤ 9.)

**The structure.** One queen per row by construction, so the recursion is over
rows and the loop is over columns. Saying that first removes half the apparent
difficulty.

**The three sets.** Derive `row - col` and `row + col` out loud. If you cannot
recall which is which, place two queens on a 3×3 board and check.

**Complexity.** Roughly O(n!) with the pruning — far better than n^n, and the
pruning is why. Be honest that the exact bound is hard to state; "bounded by
n! and much better in practice due to the constraint propagation" is the
right level of precision.

**Follow-up:** Sudoku Solver, which is the same skeleton with three
constraint sets (row, column, 3×3 box) and a "find the next empty cell"
step. If you can write N-Queens cleanly, Sudoku is transcription.
