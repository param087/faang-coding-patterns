title: The 45-minute framework
description: How to spend the clock in a coding round, and what the interviewer is scoring at each stage.

Most candidates who fail a coding round do not fail because they could not
solve the problem. They fail because they spent nine minutes silently
thinking, wrote code that did not compile, and ran out of time before testing
it.

The clock is the constraint. Here is where it goes.

## The timings

| Minutes | Stage | What you are producing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–4 | **Clarify** | constraints, edge cases, the actual question |
| 4–8 | **Brute force** | a working approach and its complexity, out loud |
| 8–14 | **Optimise** | the real approach, agreed before you type |
| 14–32 | **Code** | the implementation, narrated |
| 32–40 | **Test** | a dry run, then the edge cases |
| 40–45 | **Follow-ups** | their questions, your questions |

Meta's E4/E5 loop is a **hard stop at 35 minutes** with two problems, so halve
everything. Google gives 45 minutes and usually one or two problems with deep
follow-ups.

## 0–4 · Clarify

Ask about the input before anything else. Three questions minimum, and they
are not filler — each one changes the solution:

- **Size.** "How large can n get?" This is the single most valuable question
  on this page, because the answer tells you the complexity you can afford.
  See the [constraint cheat sheet](../constraints/).
- **Domain.** Negatives? Duplicates? Empty? Unicode or ASCII? Sorted already?
- **Output.** The value or the indices? Any valid answer or a specific one?
  What on failure — `-1`, empty, an exception?

Then **restate the problem in your own words** and get agreement. Ten seconds,
and it catches the misreading that would otherwise cost you the round.

> "So: given an unsorted array of integers that may contain duplicates and
> negatives, return the indices of the two values summing to the target, and
> there's exactly one answer. Is that right?"

## 4–8 · Brute force, with a number

State the obvious solution and **say what it costs as a number**, not as a
word:

> "The brute force is every pair, which is O(n²). At n = 10⁵ that's 10¹⁰
> operations — too slow, so I need something linear."

"Too slow" is a hedge. "10¹⁰ operations" is an argument, and it is what
justifies everything you do next. Do not skip this to look clever; skipping
straight to the optimal solution reads as memorisation, and it leaves the
interviewer nothing to grade if you then get stuck.

## 8–14 · Optimise before you type

**Do not write code until you and the interviewer agree on the approach.**
This is the most common self-inflicted wound: candidates start typing a half-
formed idea, discover the flaw at minute 25, and have no time left.

Say the approach in one or two sentences, name the complexity, and pause.

> "I'll trade space for time — as I scan, I'll check whether the complement is
> already in a hash map. One pass, O(n) time and O(n) space. Shall I code
> that?"

If they say "can you do better on space?", you have just saved yourself
fifteen minutes.

## 14–32 · Code, narrating

Write it as you would write real code. Meaningful names, no single letters
except loop indices, functions if it is long.

Narrate the *decisions*, not the syntax. Nobody needs "now I'm writing a for
loop". They do need:

> "I'm checking the complement before inserting, so a value can't pair with
> itself."

If you get stuck, **say what you are stuck on.** Silence is the worst possible
signal — the interviewer cannot help you and cannot tell whether you are
thinking or lost. "I'm trying to decide whether the left pointer should move
past the duplicate here" invites a hint and costs you nothing.

## 32–40 · Test properly

**Do not say "I think that's right".** Run the code, out loud, on a real
input. Pick one small enough to trace and interesting enough to matter — the
one that exercises the branch you were least sure of.

Then walk the edge cases explicitly:

- empty input, single element, two elements
- all identical values
- already sorted, reverse sorted
- the maximum and minimum permitted values
- negatives and zero, if allowed

Finding your own bug here is a **positive** signal. Finding it is what
engineers do; the interviewer finding it for you is not the same thing.

## 40–45 · Follow-ups

Have questions ready. They are part of the assessment, and "no, I'm good"
reads as disengagement.

Good ones: what does the team work on, what does the codebase look like, what
would the first three months involve. Bad ones: anything you could have
answered from the careers page.

## The scoring, roughly

Interviewers at every FAANG grade on approximately four axes, and coding is
only one of them:

- **Problem solving** — did you get to a good approach, and how?
- **Coding** — is it correct, clean and complete?
- **Verification** — did you test it, and did you find your own bugs?
- **Communication** — could they follow you, and could they help you?

A perfect solution delivered in silence scores worse than a good solution
delivered clearly. That is not a platitude about "soft skills" — it is
literally how the rubric is weighted, because the job involves working with
other people.

## The three ways rounds are actually lost

1. **Silent thinking.** Two minutes of quiet is fine. Nine is fatal.
2. **Coding before agreeing.** Committing to an approach nobody checked.
3. **No testing.** Running out of time with untested code on the screen, which
   the interviewer must then assume does not work.

None of the three is about algorithms.
